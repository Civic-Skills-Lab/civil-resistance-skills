# Speech Outline Scaffold

Use this reference after completing the brief. Choose the structure that fits the event, audience, and speaker. Do not force every speech into every framework. In Application Mode, the selected scaffold becomes the backbone of the full speech draft.

## Option A: Public Narrative

Best when the speech must connect values, identity, urgency, and action.

1. **Opening moment:** A concrete moment that reveals why the speaker cares.
2. **Story of self:** The speaker's stake, responsibility, or turning point.
3. **Story of us:** The shared value, experience, or constituency in the room.
4. **Challenge now:** The urgent choice, deadline, breach, or opportunity.
5. **Hope:** Why action can matter despite uncertainty.
6. **Action:** One clear next step.
7. **Closing line:** A repeatable line linking value and action.

## Option B: Civic Stump Speech

Best when the speech will be repeated across audiences and needs message discipline.

1. **Opening:** Acknowledge the audience and establish relevance.
2. **Connection:** Why the speaker belongs in this conversation.
3. **Problem:** The audience-recognized harm or unmet promise.
4. **Solution / path:** What should change and why it is credible.
5. **Vision:** What better future the action supports.
6. **Call to action:** Specific lawful next step and timing.
7. **Closing:** Thanks plus the core claim in memorable language.

## Option C: Short Public Statement

Best for press availability, public comment, vigils, brief rallies, or recorded clips.

1. **Attribution:** Name speaker, role, and standing.
2. **Point:** One-sentence claim.
3. **Proof:** One verified fact or lived example.
4. **Meaning:** Why it matters to the audience or public.
5. **Ask:** One action, request, or commitment.
6. **Boundary:** One phrase avoiding speculation or escalation if needed.

## Option D: Issue Briefing Speech

Best when the audience needs understanding before it can act.

1. **Why this matters now.**
2. **What changed or what decision is pending.**
3. **What evidence is public and reliable.**
4. **Who is affected and how.**
5. **What choice the audience or institution faces.**
6. **What action follows.**

## Drafting Rules

- Write for listening, not reading: shorter sentences, clear transitions, and one idea per beat.
- Keep the central claim repeatable without context.
- Use evidence to support the story; do not bury the story under facts.
- Add explicit transitions where the speech moves from story to evidence, evidence to claim, and claim to action.
- Read the outline aloud before drafting; cut beats that cannot be spoken naturally within the time limit.
- Avoid jokes unless the speaker can deliver them safely and the topic permits it.
- Prefer a memorized outline over brittle word-for-word delivery when context may shift.
- Prepare one short version and one expanded version if the event timing is uncertain.
