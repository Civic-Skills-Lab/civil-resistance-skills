# Research the Audience

## Purpose

Before any public-facing tactic, map the audience whose perception the action is meant to shift. Audience research determines speaker attribution, venue choice, timing, amplification strategy, and the specific claim to centre. A tactic designed for the wrong audience signals well but lands nowhere.

## Key patterns

- **Name at least three audience layers.** (1) The *immediate* participants and in-person audience — what they already believe and what they are there to validate; (2) the *secondary* audience reached via documentation, press, and social amplification — who hears about the event without witnessing it; (3) the *pillar* audience — the specific institutions or constituencies whose support shift the campaign is optimising for (security services, bureaucracy, business, media, religious leadership, international allies). Each layer reads the same event differently.
- **Segment within layers.** Within each layer distinguish committed supporters (already mobilised, need reinforcement), fence-sitters (persuadable, need legible evidence of reasonableness and scale), and hostile observers (will read the event through an adversarial frame — shape preparation to pre-empt that frame).
- **Identify the marginal persuadable.** Most campaign movement happens at the fence-sitter edge, not the committed core. Ask: whose opinion, if shifted, would change the balance? Design the tactic around that segment's criteria for legitimacy.
- **Test your audience model.** Before committing, run the planned tactic past two or three people who match the target audience but are not inside the organising group. Note where their reading diverges from the organisers' — that divergence is where preparation should invest.
- **Keep the model written.** A one-page audience map — layers, segments, marginal-persuadable profile, known pre-existing frames — is the single most reused preparation artefact across tactics. Maintain it; update after each action based on actual reception.

## Common failure modes

- Designing for the committed core and confusing turnout for persuasion — most people already agreed before the event.
- Treating "the public" as one audience. There is no single public; there are many audiences reading the same event through different priors.
- Ignoring hostile observers. Opposing outlets and commentators will cover the event; their frame is part of the outcome. Research their likely frame before the event, not after.
- Skipping the pillar layer. Campaign-level leverage comes from pillar shifts (Helvey's pillars-of-support framework); ignoring how a tactic reads to pillars treats the tactic as an end rather than a means.
- One-shot research. The audience is not static — a single audience map at kickoff decays; update after each action.
