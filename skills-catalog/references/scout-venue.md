# Scout the Venue

## Purpose

Walk the physical or digital space where the action will happen before the date, at the same time of day. On-the-ground reality routinely diverges from the map or the platform preview — chokepoints, sightlines, access patterns, and exit options become visible only on foot. Venue scouting is the single most-cited preparation step across documented cases; skipping it accounts for a disproportionate share of preventable failures.

## Key patterns

- **Match the scout to the event.** A venue scouted at 9am does not reliably predict 6pm foot traffic, crowd flow, lighting, or enforcement posture. Scout in conditions that match the planned action.
- **Map four categories of feature.** (1) Start point — room for assembly, sign distribution, sound check; (2) Movement path or working space — chokepoints, narrow passages, surfaces that affect visibility; (3) Vantage points — where documentarians will stand, where press will gather, where opposing observers can surveil; (4) Exit options — at least two independent routes for ordinary dispersal and one fallback for pressure.
- **Log features in writing.** A shared map with annotated photos survives hand-offs between scouts and day-of coordinators far better than memory.
- **Note legal frontage.** Businesses, embassies, residences adjacent to the route or venue shape legal and narrative risk — confrontation with a specific business captures coverage and recontextualises the action. Scout their frontage.
- **Check amplification feasibility.** Acoustics, cell coverage for livestream, line-of-sight to broadcast cameras. If any fails, pick a different venue or plan workarounds.
- **Re-scout if conditions shift.** Construction, weather, a counter-event announcement, a changed enforcement policy — any of these invalidate a scout from even two weeks ago. Re-walk.

## Common failure modes

- Relying only on satellite imagery or platform previews. Ground-level constraints — parked vehicles blocking sightlines, surveillance camera placement, narrow sidewalks — are invisible from the map.
- Scouting at the wrong time of day. Weekday morning routes behave differently at weekend afternoon; rush hour reshapes chokepoints.
- Single-scout bias. One scout misses features another would catch. Two independent scouts, ideally including someone not inside the organising group, reveal blind spots.
- Skipping the endpoint. The dispersal geography is often where things go wrong; scout it with as much care as the assembly area.
- Ignoring adjacent communities or businesses whose consent is not secured. A route running past a mosque during Friday prayer, or through a residential block at 10pm, invites counter-framing.
- No re-scout after announcement. Opposing preparation starts when the action is publicised; the venue may look different a day before than it did a month before.
